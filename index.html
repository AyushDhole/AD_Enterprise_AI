Enterprise AI

Software that stopped waiting for a click and started finishing the task.

A company that buys AI needs more than a model. It needs clean data, a way to check the work, a price that makes sense and someone to sign off. This is my read of where the money is going, what keeps breaking, and where a small early-stage fund can still find an edge.

The Neon lens

Startups to track

Neon backs India and diaspora founders building enterprise AI for global buyers, mostly at pre-seed and seed. First cheques are reported at $100K to $500K, and the reported goal is $10M ARR in three to five years.8 I left out the well-known names on purpose and looked for early teams spread across the stack.

How I screen: pilot-to-paid rate, gross margin at 10x volume, how much revenue is really services, and whether the moat is workflow, data or trust rather than access to a model. Details here come from public press and databases, not Neon’s pipeline, and stages go out of date quickly, so check before relying on any of them.

If I joined

My first 90 days

Weeks 1 to 3

Map the space

Build a map of 60 to 80 early companies across data and context, agent tooling and vertical back offices. Track stage, last round, named customers and any sign of pilot-to-paid conversion. Refresh it every two weeks.

Weeks 3 to 8

Talk to buyers

Speak to about 15 US buyers, such as security leads, operations heads and platform engineers. Ask what they tried, what stalled and what they would pay for.

Weeks 6 to 12

Measure the portfolio

Collect three numbers from every portfolio company: pilot-to-paid rate, gross margin at 10x volume and share of revenue from services. Patterns across companies say more than any single pitch.

The honest gap: everything in this brief comes from public data and launches. The buyer conversations in step two are the part it is missing, and the first thing I would do is test these views against them and against Neon’s own deal flow.

Closing note

What I took away

Thoughts on enterprise AI, by Ayush Dhole

The thing that stayed with me while reading is how little of the story is about models. Models got good quickly. What is slow is everything around them: getting the data in, getting a security team to say yes, and showing a finance head that the work was done right.

That is where I think small teams still have room. Trust tooling and data plumbing are less crowded than they look from outside, and so is the one painful back-office job that a founder understands better than anyone. A team like that can get a customer to pay before the big platforms notice.

I also know what this work is missing. It is built from public data, and the next step is to sit with buyers and find out where I am wrong. That is the part I would most like to do at Neon.

Ayush Dhole
